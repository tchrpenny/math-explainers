Try–Discuss–Connect in K–1

The routine is the same as in the upper grades, but kindergarten and first grade run it shorter, more out loud, and more hands-on. Tap each stage below and try its quick activity.

The three stages

Sample problem (kindergarten, adding within 5):

3 frogs are on a log. 2 more frogs hop on. How many frogs are on the log now? How can you show it?
Try It · about 7 min

Children figure out what the story is about, then solve it on their own with any tool they choose. The struggle is the point, so a partial answer is fine.

Children

  • Retell the story in their own words
  • Choose a tool: fingers, cubes, counters, a drawing
  • Show the total in their own way

You

  • Read the problem aloud using the language routine (Three Reads, Say It Another Way)
  • Give quiet think time and resist showing a strategy
  • Circulate and jot down who did what. This is your sharing plan.

Say this, not that

Overscaffolding usually sounds helpful. Here's how to swap it out:

Not: "Get 3 cubes. Now get 2 more. Count them all."
Say: "What is happening in the story? What could you use to show the frogs?"
Not: Modeling the strategy on the board before children try
Do: Keep tools within reach and ask, "Have you tried using your fingers or cubes?"
Not: Waiting until every child has the answer
Do: Move on when most children have a start. Their partial work fuels the discussion.
Discuss It · about 7 min

Partners explain to each other first. Then you choose 2–3 children and decide the order they share, so the discussion builds from concrete to abstract.

Children

  • Partner A shares first, then Partner B
  • Use a frame: "I used ___ to show ___."
  • Show agreement or disagreement with hand signals

You

  • Name who shares first in each pair
  • Pick and order the sharers before calling the class together
  • Ask, "How does [name]'s way show the total?"

Connect It · about 6 min

Bridge what children did to the math idea and its words. Pick the one or two questions that confirm today's purpose. This is a conversation, not a page to fill in.

Children

  • Say how their way matches a classmate's or the book's model
  • Use the new word in a sentence: "The total is 5."
  • Move into the Apply It activity

You

  • Ask 1–2 Teacher's Guide questions, chosen from what you heard
  • Name the vocabulary as children use it
  • Grade 1: if no one made the Model It model, connect it to theirs

Teach vocabulary in context

Tap a moment to see how to bring in the word total.

Words stick when children meet them while they're doing the math, not in a list beforehand.

The 20% that gives 80% of the payoff

  • Protect think time. Read aloud, ask "what's happening?", then step back. No modeling the strategy.
  • Plan the share while you circulate. Choose 2–3 children and an order, from concrete to more abstract.
  • Ask 1–2 questions in Connect It, then move on. The goal is the session purpose, not a finished page.

Common slips

Overscaffolding during Try It

Telling children which tool to use or walking them step by step takes away the thinking. Everyone ends up with the same strategy, and Discuss It has nothing to compare.

Instead: Ask open questions ("What could you use to show it?"), keep tools within reach, and accept partial work.

Calling on volunteers instead of sequencing

Whoever raises a hand first sets the direction. The class may jump straight to the most efficient way and never see how the strategies connect.

Instead: Use your circulation notes. Choose sharers and an order, and tell each child ahead of time: "I'd love for you to share your cubes."

Teaching vocabulary apart from the math

Front-loading a word list before the problem means words without meaning.

Instead: Name the word as it happens ("You found the total!"), post sentence frames that use it, and ask children to say it back.

Treating the workbook as the goal

Completing every item on the page eats up the 6 minutes for Connect It and turns discussion into dictation.

Instead: Pick 1–2 questions that match the session purpose. Discuss them, or have children draw or write. Skip the rest.
